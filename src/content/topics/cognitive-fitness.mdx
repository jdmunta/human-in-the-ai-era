<Lede>
For most of the twentieth century, physical labour was something you were
subjected to. Then machines took it, and within two generations we had invented
an entire industry devoted to voluntarily lifting heavy objects for no reason.
This looks absurd until you understand it as the correct response: when a
capacity stops being maintained by necessity, it must be maintained on purpose,
or it goes. The same logic is about to apply to the mind, and the equivalent
institutions do not exist yet.
</Lede>

## Offloading is not new and mostly not bad

The alarm about cognitive outsourcing is at least as old as literacy. In the
*Phaedrus*, Plato has Socrates complain that writing will "create forgetfulness
in the learners' souls," producing people who appear knowledgeable while knowing
nothing. He was, on the narrow point, correct — we did stop memorising epics — and
on the broad point spectacularly wrong.

The pattern has repeated with maps, printing, arithmetic, calculators, and
search. In each case a capacity was surrendered and something larger was
purchased with the surrender. Cognitive scientists call the general phenomenon
**cognitive offloading**, and the position that this is normal rather than
pathological has strong support: Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis argues
that the notebook in your pocket is genuinely part of your cognitive system, not
a crutch attached to it.

<Sidenote>
The "Google effect" (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, 2011): people who expect to be able
to look something up recall the fact less well but recall *where to find it*
better. Memory reorganised rather than degraded — a transactive system, not a
broken one.
</Sidenote>

So the question is not whether to offload. You already do, constantly, and
should. The question is which offloads are cheap and which are expensive.

Most public argument about AI and the mind fails because it treats all offloading
as equivalent — either all fine (calculators!) or all catastrophic (Plato!). The
useful position is neither. It is that a specific, identifiable minority of
cognitive skills are both self-verifying and generative, and those are the ones
requiring deliberate maintenance. Everything else can go, with thanks.

<Claim label="The claim">
Writing is the load-bearing case. Writing is not the transcription of thought
that already exists; it is the process by which thought becomes precise enough to
be wrong. Offload the prose and you have not saved time on expressing your
thinking — you have skipped the step where the thinking would have happened, and
received something fluent in its place. This is the most consequential cheap
trade available today, and it is being made everywhere, by default, silently.
</Claim>

## Desirable difficulty

Robert Bjork's research programme produced a finding that is genuinely
counterintuitive and extremely well replicated: **conditions that make learning
feel harder and slower often produce better long-term retention and transfer**.
Spacing beats massing. Interleaved practice beats blocked practice. Retrieving
from memory beats re-reading. Generating an answer beats being shown one.

He called these *desirable difficulties*, and the crucial part is the perceptual
trap that comes with them: learners consistently rate the harder conditions as
less effective while performing better on them later. Our sense of how well we
are learning is systematically inverted.

<Sidenote>
Which means "this tool makes learning feel effortless" is, on the evidence, a
warning label rather than a feature description.
</Sidenote>

Every AI interface is optimised to remove friction. That is what makes them good
products. It also means they are optimised, without intending to be, against the
exact conditions under which humans learn best. There is no villain here — just a
misalignment between what a good interface does and what a growing mind needs.

## What a regime looks like

Borrow the structure from physical training, because it transfers surprisingly
well.

- **Specificity.** Train the capacity you want to keep. If you want to keep
  reasoning, do reasoning — not puzzle apps. The transfer literature on
  general "brain training" is discouraging; the literature on domain practice is
  not.
- **Progressive overload.** The difficulty has to increase or adaptation stops.
  Re-reading familiar material is the cognitive equivalent of lifting the same
  light weight forever.
- **Recovery.** Consolidation happens in the gaps — sleep, walks, the shower.
  Filling every interstitial moment with input is over-training.
- **Consistency over intensity.** Thirty minutes daily beats one heroic Sunday,
  for the same reasons it does in the gym.

<Counter label="The strongest objection">
"You are prescribing artificial hardship. Nobody insists on hand-washing clothes
to preserve their scrubbing skills, and the mental capacities you want to
maintain are simply obsolete in the same way. Effort is not intrinsically
virtuous. If a tool gets me a better result faster, spending an hour reaching a
worse result unaided is not training — it is a hobby with a moral justification
attached."
</Counter>

Half right, and the half it gets right is important: effort has no intrinsic
value, and cognitive-fitness talk slides easily into puritanism. But the
laundry analogy relies on scrubbing being non-generative and self-verifying —
clean clothes are clean regardless of who cleaned them. The claim here is
narrow and testable: for the small set of skills that are *both* generative
*and* required to evaluate their own output, the trade is not free, and the
cost is paid later, invisibly, at exactly the moment you most need the
capability. Everything outside that set — automate it without guilt.
