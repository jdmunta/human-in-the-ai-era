<Lede>
The question arrives in a natural order. If machines can do the work, does
anyone need a job? And if nobody needs a job, what exactly is a Senior Director
of anything — what is the title for, and why is one salary four times another?
The intuition behind the question is sound. The answer is that a job was never
one thing, and the part automation takes is not the part titles were ever really
about.
</Lede>

Most discussion collapses all five into the first. That is why "AI will do the
work, so jobs are over" feels obviously true and turns out to be only
one-fifth true.

## What a title actually encodes

Titles look like rewards, which is why they look absurd once the work is
automated. But reward is the least of what they do. Strip the ceremony and a
title is doing four jobs at once:

- **Routing.** It tells everyone else where a decision goes. An organisation
  running mostly on machines still has to answer "who decides this?" — arguably
  more urgently, because decisions arrive faster.
- **Answerability.** It names who is on the hook. This is the one that cannot be
  automated, delegated or distributed, because the whole point is that a
  specific person can be asked to explain.
- **Standing to refuse.** A title is, functionally, the authority to say no and
  survive it. The [three-question test](/topics/responsibility) fails without it
  — you get responsibility with no power, which is a crumple zone.
- **External commitment.** Counterparties need to know whose signature binds the
  organisation. Nobody signs a contract with a workflow.

<Sidenote>
Notice how many of these are about <em>refusal</em> rather than production. The
authority to stop something is the part of a senior role that was always
underdescribed, and it is the part that is about to be all that is left.
</Sidenote>

<Claim label="The claim">
Titles survive to the extent that they encode accountability and decision
rights. They erode to the extent that they encode volume of output supervised.
“Manages a team of twenty” means progressively less when the twenty are agents.
“The person who signs, and who could have refused” means progressively more. A
hierarchy of throughput is being replaced by a hierarchy of answerability — and
those are not the same people.
</Claim>

## What happens to salary

Wages have always been three things bundled: the value of what you produce, your
bargaining power, and rents — whatever you can capture from position, scarcity
or convention. Automation changes the mix rather than deleting the total.

- **Pay for doing falls.** If the marginal product of an hour of your cognitive
  labour drops, so does the price of that hour. There is no way around this.
- **Pay for deciding, bearing risk and being answerable holds or rises.** These
  are exactly the things that cannot be handed to a system that has nothing to
  lose.
- **The <em>form</em> of pay is the fragile part.** Salary is compensation for
  time — an arrangement that made sense when output scaled with hours. As it
  stops doing that, compensation drifts toward the shapes used for people who
  are paid for judgment and exposure rather than attendance: equity, profit
  share, retainers, fees per decision, partnership. That is how partners,
  founders and licensed professionals have always been paid, and it is a better
  model for what senior work is becoming.

So the honest prediction is not "salaries become meaningless." It is that
**payment for time becomes meaningless, and payment for exposure does not** —
and that the people who currently hold titles are not uniformly the people who
will hold exposure.

## Does anyone need a job?

Two different questions live inside this one, and they have opposite answers.

**Does society need every adult in a job?** Probably not, and increasingly not.
What a society needs is that certain functions have owners: someone accountable
for consequential decisions, someone stewarding institutions, someone doing the
irreducibly physical and relational work. That is a large number of people. It is
not everybody, and the gap is what the
[civilization](/topics/civilization) page is about.

**Do you need what a job supplies?** Almost certainly yes — and this is where the
intuition "no need for jobs" quietly overreaches. Marienthal is the evidence:
when work disappeared, the wage was the most replaceable of the losses.
Structure, contact, collective purpose, status and the sense of being depended
upon are separate goods, and a world that supplies income while supplying none of
them produces people who are comfortable and unwell. See
[meaning](/topics/meaning).

The correct formulation is narrow: **you may not need a job; you do need the
things a job happened to bundle.** They can be obtained elsewhere. They will not
be obtained by accident.

## The part nobody enjoys

Here is the uncomfortable half of the answer, and it is the reason "abolish
titles" is not a plan.

Status is **positional**. It is not a quantity a society can decide to stop
producing; it is a ranking, and rankings persist as long as people care about
where they stand — which is to say, always. Remove one ladder and you do not get
a flat society. You get a different ladder, allocated by whatever is next in
line: proximity to capital, credentials, tenure, family, charisma, political
skill inside the organisation.

Those substitutes are, on the whole, *worse* than the one being displaced.
Contribution-based hierarchy has real problems, but it is at least nominally
legible and nominally earnable. "Who is close to the people who own the compute"
is neither.

<Sidenote>
This is the strongest practical argument for the
<a href="/topics/civilization">civilization</a> page's fourth lever. If status
is going to be allocated regardless, allocating it deliberately — toward care,
stewardship, teaching, repair — is not sentimentality. It is choosing which
ladder replaces the one that is going.
</Sidenote>

<Counter label="The strongest objection">
"This is an elaborate rationalisation of hierarchy. If machines do the work,
then titles and pay differentials are rent extraction with a story attached —
the senior person is not ten times more accountable, they are simply better
placed. The honest response is not to redesign the ladder but to flatten it and
distribute the output, and every argument that accountability requires
seniority has historically been made by the senior."
</Counter>

Substantially fair on rents: a great deal of what titles currently purchase is
positional and would not survive scrutiny. But the objection conflates two
things that behave differently. **Income can be distributed; answerability
cannot.** You can pay everyone the same and still need one named person who can
be asked why a decision was made — and if that person lacks the standing to
refuse, you have not flattened the hierarchy, you have only removed the
compensation for standing in it. The reform this argument actually supports is
narrower and harder than abolition: make titles track real decision rights and
real liability, and pay for exposure rather than for position. Flattening
without that produces organisations where nobody is answerable, which is not a
more equal arrangement — it is an unaccountable one.
