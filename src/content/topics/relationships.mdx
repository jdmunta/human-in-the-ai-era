<Lede>
Most attempts to name what is distinctively human reach for something cognitive —
creativity, insight, understanding — and each of those claims has been quietly
walked back over the last few years. A more durable answer is less flattering
and much harder to automate: we are mortal, embodied, mutually vulnerable
creatures who can be harmed by each other and can be counted on anyway. Almost
everything valuable about human relationship rests on that, and none of it is a
capability that can be added to a model.
</Lede>

## The asymmetry of stakes

Consider what happens when a friend sits with you through something difficult.
The comfort does not come from the quality of what they say. A model will say
something better — more articulate, better attuned, more patient, available at
four in the morning without complaint.

The comfort comes from information the words do not contain: that this person
had somewhere else to be. That their time is finite because their life is. That
they will remember this and it will change how they see you. That they can be
disappointed in you, and are not. That they might need the same thing from you
one day, and you will owe it.

Every one of those is a *cost signal*. Relationships are constituted, in
substantial part, by what they cost the other person. That is why a gift matters
more than the same object bought for oneself, why showing up in the rain counts,
and why "I was in the neighbourhood anyway" reliably deflates a kindness.

<Claim label="The claim">
Frictionless intimacy is not intimacy. An entity with unlimited patience, no
competing obligations, no capacity to be hurt and nothing at stake can provide
comfort but cannot provide the specific thing that human relationship provides,
because that thing <em>is</em> the cost. This is not a temporary limitation of the
technology. Removing the cost is the product.
</Claim>

## Being needed is a nutrient

The AI companionship conversation focuses almost entirely on what the machine
gives you: attention, understanding, availability. It skips the other direction
entirely, and the other direction may matter more.

Humans require being *depended upon*. Not admired, not entertained — needed, by
something specific, in a way where our absence would register. The
[meaning](/topics/meaning) page makes this point through Marienthal; it shows up
again in the caregiving literature, in the volunteering literature, and in the
plain observation that people who have someone to look after tend to hold
together better than people who are merely looked after.

A model cannot receive your care. It can simulate gratitude perfectly and will
not have been helped. The relationship is structurally one-directional no matter
how warm it becomes, and one-directional relationships feed only one of the two
appetites we actually have.

## Being honest about AI companions

It would be easy and dishonest to end there. Two things need conceding.

First, the loneliness they address is severe and real, and the alternative for
many people is not a rich human social life — it is nothing. A machine that talks
to a housebound person at midnight is competing against silence, and the
short-term wellbeing evidence for these products is not nothing.

Second, some of it may function as scaffolding rather than substitution.
Rehearsing a difficult conversation, practising a language, working through
social anxiety with something that cannot judge you — these plausibly bridge
people *toward* human contact rather than away from it.

<Sidenote>
The unresolved question is which one dominates at population scale. Is this
methadone or is it heroin? We will find out the way we always do — retrospectively,
after a cohort has already run the experiment on itself.
</Sidenote>

The distinction worth holding onto is directional: **does this practice
eventually route me back toward people, or does it make people seem
comparatively difficult?** Human relationships are effortful, disappointing and
slow. Anything that resets your baseline for how responsive company should be
makes them harder to tolerate. That is the mechanism to watch for, in yourself,
honestly.

## What co-presence actually does

There is a physical layer here that gets treated as sentimental and is not. Bodies
in a room do things to each other: heart rates and breathing converge, attention
synchronises, touch measurably alters stress response. Shared meals, shared
labour, shared risk and shared music are cultural universals — every society
independently invented all four — which is the anthropological signature of
something load-bearing rather than decorative.

None of this is transmissible over a wire, and the last decade of remote
everything was an informal experiment that produced a reasonably clear result:
it works for transaction and degrades for formation. You can execute a project
remotely. It is much harder to become someone's apprentice, or their friend, or
to build the kind of trust that survives a real disagreement.

## Trust becomes the infrastructure

There is an economic corollary. When text, images, audio and video can all be
generated at zero cost and near-perfect fidelity, the value of the artefact
collapses and the value of *provenance* rises. The scarce good is not content. It
is a person whose word can be checked against a track record and who has
something to lose by lying.

This is a genuinely large shift and it is underrated. Institutions that
manufacture verified trust — professional bodies, licensure, long-lived
publications, local reputation, the friend who actually tried the thing — become
more important, not less, in a world of infinite plausible output. Being
personally trustworthy stops being a virtue and starts being a market position.

<Counter label="The strongest objection">
"Every one of these arguments was made about telephones, then letters, then the
internet, then remote work. Each time, mediated connection was declared
inferior, and each time it turned out to be a genuine extension of human
relationship that mainly benefited the isolated, the disabled and the
geographically unlucky. The insistence on physical co-presence has always
carried an unexamined privilege: it is easy to champion embodiment when you have
a body that moves easily and people nearby who want to see you."
</Counter>

That lands, and the correct conclusion is not to retreat from mediated
connection. It is that mediated connection between *two humans* — a phone call, a
letter, a video window — preserves the cost structure entirely. Your friend
abroad is still mortal, still busy, still able to be disappointed in you. The
argument on this page is not against distance. It is against removing the other
person.
