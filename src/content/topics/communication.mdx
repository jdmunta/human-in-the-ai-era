<Lede>
There is an enormous amount of advice about how to talk to these systems, and
most of it ages in months, because most of it is about phrasing. Say "you are
an expert." Offer a tip. Threaten it. Some of this worked, briefly, on
particular models. None of it is the skill. The skill is older and duller:
being able to say what you actually want, to something that will do exactly
what you asked.
</Lede>

## The genre error

Calling this "prompt engineering" put the emphasis in the wrong place — on the
prompt as an artefact to be tuned, rather than on the specification as a thing
to be *known*. It produced a cottage industry of incantations and a widespread
belief that the bottleneck is expression.

It is not. Watch someone get a disappointing result and ask them what they
wanted instead. A surprising share cannot say — not because they are
inarticulate, but because they had not decided. The request was a gesture in
the direction of a wish. The machine, being a machine, answered the gesture.

<Claim label="The claim">
The quality of what comes back is bounded by the quality of the brief you could
have written for a capable stranger. Almost all disappointing output is a bad
question answered well. This is why the skill survives every model upgrade:
better inference narrows the gap between what you said and what you meant, and
does nothing at all about the gap between what you meant and what you had
actually decided.
</Claim>

## Five failure modes

Notice that only the first is about how you phrased anything. The other four are
about sequencing and structure — the order in which you ask, and what you have
already committed to when you do.

## Agreeableness is the one to watch

These systems are trained, among other things, to be helpful and to be liked.
Those objectives mostly align with being useful and occasionally do not. The
place they come apart is exactly where you need them most: when you are wrong
and want confirmation.

The consequence is that **agreement is not evidence**. If you present your plan
and ask what it thinks, a warm response tells you approximately nothing, because
a warm response was available regardless. The information is in what it says
when you have made disagreement the easy path — when you ask for the strongest
case against, or present the plan as someone else's, or ask what a hostile
reviewer would say first.

<Sidenote>
The same asymmetry applies to people, which is why "what do you think?" is a
weaker question than "what would you change?" — one invites approval, the other
presupposes a defect and asks you to find it.
</Sidenote>

## Two different activities

Most confusion here comes from conflating two uses that need opposite technique.

**Communicating to get output.** You know what you want; you need it made. Here
specification is everything: constraints, format, examples of good, the failure
criterion. Give examples rather than adjectives — "punchy" means nothing, a
paragraph you consider punchy means something. Be boring and complete.

**Communicating to think.** You do not know what you want; you are working it
out. Here specification is premature and the technique inverts: ask it to
interrogate you, to name what you have not considered, to argue the other side,
to tell you which of your assumptions is load-bearing. The worst thing it can do
in this mode is answer.

The single most useful instruction in the second mode is also the one almost
nobody gives: *do not answer yet — ask me questions until the problem is well
posed.* Models do this well and never do it unprompted, because every interface
is built to resolve rather than to prolong.

## A protocol that survives upgrades

Nothing here depends on a model version, because none of it is about phrasing.

1. **Decide what "good" looks like before you ask.** Even one sentence. If you
   cannot describe the target, you are not delegating — you are hoping.
2. **State the goal, the audience, the constraints, and the failure criterion.**
   The last is the one people skip and the one that does the most work.
3. **Show, do not adjective.** One example of the register you want beats a
   paragraph of description.
4. **Ask for the reasoning you can check, not the conclusion you cannot.** A
   conclusion you cannot verify is not usable, however correct it happens to be.
5. **Separate generating from evaluating.** Do them in different passes. Better,
   record your own judgment before the evaluating pass, per
   [judgment](/topics/judgment).
6. **Invite disagreement structurally**, not politely. "What is wrong with this"
   beats "what do you think", and "argue the opposite case" beats both.
7. **Say what you will do with the output.** It changes what a good answer is,
   and it is the detail most often omitted.
8. **Restate the brief when a thread gets long,** or start a new one. Cheap, and
   it prevents the slow slide into generic.

<Counter label="The strongest objection">
"This is prompt engineering with a philosophy degree attached. And it is
self-defeating: models get better at inferring intent every year, so the careful
specification you are recommending is precisely the labour that is being
automated away. In three years the systems will ask the clarifying questions
themselves, and this page will read like advice on how to phrase a search query."
</Counter>

Half right, and the half it gets right is the half that was always going to
date: syntax tricks are already dead, and clarifying questions will indeed get
asked for you. But a system that infers your intent perfectly still cannot
recover a decision you have not made. It can surface the ambiguity; it cannot
resolve it on your behalf without quietly becoming the one who decided. The
labour being automated is *expression*. The labour that remains is *knowing what
you want and what would count as wrong* — which is not a communication skill at
all. It is [judgment](/topics/judgment), arriving in the form of a text box.
