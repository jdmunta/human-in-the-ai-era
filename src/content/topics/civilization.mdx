<Lede>
This is the page where writing about AI usually becomes unfalsifiable. Words
like *flourishing*, *consciousness* and *the next stage of humanity* arrive, and
the reader is left with a warm feeling and nothing to do on Monday. So there is
one rule here: no civilizational claim without a mechanism. If it cannot be
stated as something specific that someone could adjust, it does not belong on
the page.
</Lede>

Four things are genuinely adjustable. They are boring compared to the usual
register, which is the point.

## Lever one: what we measure

GDP counts production. It has no opinion about whether the producing was done by
people, and no way to represent whether those people retained any agency in the
process. A society that doubled its output while its citizens lost the capacity
to direct their own lives would register an unambiguous boom.

This is not a new complaint — Kuznets, who built the accounts, warned about it in
1934 — but it becomes urgent when output and human involvement decouple. If the
dashboard cannot see the thing that is being lost, the loss is invisible to
policy until it is irreversible.

The serious alternative is Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum's **capabilities
approach**: measure not what is produced, nor even what people have, but what
people are actually *able to do and be*. It is already operationalised — the UN
Human Development Index descends from it, as does New Zealand's wellbeing
budgeting and Bhutan's much-mocked and much-more-rigorous-than-you-think GNH
framework.

What an AI-era capability dashboard would have to track, that current ones do
not:

- **Time sovereignty.** What share of waking hours are self-directed? Abundance
  that arrives as more monitored work is not abundance.
- **Agency in consequential decisions.** Can people meaningfully contest a
  decision made about them — a loan, a diagnosis, a sentence, a hiring? Contest,
  not be informed of.
- **Skill retention at population level.** Are people becoming more or less able
  to act without assistance? Nobody measures this. It is the most important
  indicator on the list and there is no series for it.
- **Depth of social connection.** Not headcount. Number of people who would
  notice your absence within a week.

<Sidenote>
The general rule of institutional design: you get what you measure, and you get
it at the expense of what you do not. Choosing the indicators is not preparatory
work before the real decisions. It is most of the decision.
</Sidenote>

## Lever two: how we distribute

If capital produces an increasing share of output, income concentrates among
capital owners by default. Nothing about this is mysterious and nothing about it
is self-correcting.

But the [meaning](/topics/meaning) page constrains the solution space in a way
that most distribution debates ignore. Marienthal showed that the deprivations of
worklessness are only partly financial: time structure, social contact,
collective purpose and status are separate goods that money does not
automatically buy. **A pure cash transfer addresses one of five deprivations.**

The honest conclusion is that no single mechanism does the whole job, and the
debate's fixation on UBI-versus-nothing is a failure of imagination about which
deprivation is being addressed.

<Claim label="The claim">
The distribution problem and the meaning problem are usually treated as
sequential — first solve income, then worry about purpose. They are the same
problem. Any mechanism that supplies money while supplying no structure, no
collective purpose and no status will produce a materially comfortable
population that is, by every measure we have, unwell. Marienthal is the warning,
and it is a hundred years old.
</Claim>

## Lever three: how we decide

Two pressures arrive together and point in opposite directions.

Decisions are being made faster than deliberation can track — by automated
systems, at machine speed, in trading, moderation, targeting, logistics and
increasingly in administration. Meanwhile the *quality* of public deliberation is
under direct attack from cheap, personalised, industrial-scale persuasion.

Three mechanisms with actual track records are worth more than any amount of
speculation:

- **Sortition and citizens' assemblies.** Randomly selected citizens, given time,
  expert briefing and skilled facilitation, deliberating on one hard question.
  Ireland used this to break a thirty-year deadlock on constitutional questions
  that its parliament could not touch. It works because random selection cannot
  be lobbied and cannot be targeted by a persuasion campaign that does not know
  who will be picked.
- **Mandatory contestability.** Not "explainability" in the abstract, but a
  concrete right: any consequential automated decision about a person must come
  with a route to human review that has the power to overturn it. The
  [responsibility](/topics/responsibility) page's three-question test is the
  design specification — information, time, standing.
- **Audit before deployment, in public.** The model aviation and pharmaceuticals
  arrived at after enough disasters: independent pre-deployment testing,
  mandatory incident reporting, published investigations. It is expensive and
  slow and it is the only regulatory pattern that has ever demonstrably worked
  on a complex technical system.

## Lever four: what we honour

This is the most underrated lever because it looks unserious. It is not. Status
is the currency human societies actually run on, and status hierarchies are
*designed* — usually implicitly, occasionally on purpose, and they do move.
Duelling was high-status and now looks insane. Smoking was sophisticated. Drunk
driving was a joke. Each shifted within a generation, through deliberate
campaigns, and the shift changed behaviour far more than the law did.

We currently award status overwhelmingly for economic productivity. That
allocation was reasonable when production was the binding constraint. If
production stops being the constraint and human agency becomes it, then
continuing to award status for output is a design error with severe
consequences — it means the highest-status activity available is one people are
being progressively excluded from.

The activities worth deliberately elevating are not hard to identify. They are
the ones that are already essential, already unautomatable, and already
low-status: **care, teaching, repair, maintenance, stewardship, and the
restraint to not do something you could do.** Every one of them is a
[stewardship](/topics/purpose) role. Every one is undervalued precisely because
it produces no artefact.

## The Illich test

Ivan Illich, in *Tools for Conviviality* (1973), proposed the sharpest single
question available for evaluating a technology, and it is more useful now than
when he wrote it.

> A convivial tool expands the range of what people can do for themselves. A
> manipulative one makes them dependent on a system they do not control and
> cannot inspect.
>
> <cite>After Illich, Tools for Conviviality</cite>

The same technology can be either, depending on how it is deployed. A model that
teaches you to reason is convivial. The same model, delivered as an oracle whose
reasoning you cannot inspect and whose availability you do not control, is not.
The difference is not in the weights. It is in the interface, the ownership
structure and the incentives of whoever runs it — all of which are choices, and
all of which are currently being made by a very small number of organisations
with no obligation to consider the question.

## Three failure modes, named plainly

- **Capture.** The productivity gains accrue to a narrow group. Historically the
  default outcome of a general-purpose technology, and it takes decades of
  political conflict to broaden. This one is not a risk; it is the baseline you
  have to actively deviate from.
- **Comfortable irrelevance.** Material abundance combined with mass agency loss.
  Nothing hurts, nothing depends on anyone, and the population declines in a way
  that no economic indicator can see. This is the specifically novel risk — every
  previous civilisational failure mode involved scarcity.
- **Deliberative collapse.** Decision speed and persuasion capability outrun
  collective reasoning, so governance loses the ability to steer at exactly the
  moment steering matters most.

<Counter label="The strongest objection">
"Civilizations are not designed, they emerge. Every attempt to consciously
redesign one — from the Five-Year Plan to the model city — has produced worse
outcomes than the messy adaptation it replaced. The Enlightenment was not
planned. The industrial transition was not steered; it was survived, and then
tidied up afterwards by reformers responding to specific visible harms. This
page is a wish list with a mechanism section stapled on."
</Counter>

The historical record supports the objection about *comprehensive* design and not
at all about *specific institutional mechanisms*. Nobody designed the industrial
economy. People absolutely did design the factory inspectorate, the eight-hour
day, universal schooling, the pension, the antibiotic approval process and the
air-accident investigation bureau — each a targeted response to an identified
failure, each contested, each transformative. That is the register this page is
in. Not a blueprint for civilization. A short list of adjustable things, argued
one at a time.
