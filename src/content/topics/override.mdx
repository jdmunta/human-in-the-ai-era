<Lede>
The worry is that a machine will make a decision that harms someone, and that
no human will have been able to stop it. This is a reasonable thing to be
afraid of and a difficult thing to act on, because the sentence hides at least
six different situations. They have different mechanisms, different warning
signs and different fixes — and a countermeasure aimed at the wrong one provides
the feeling of safety without any of it.
</Lede>

## The six

Notice that only the second is about an individual failing, and only the fifth
is about skill. Four of the six are properties of how a system was *built and
deployed* — which means most of the override problem is a governance question
that has been quietly reassigned to whoever happens to be holding the mouse.

<Claim label="The claim">
Almost every real override happens through mechanisms one, three and six —
authority removed at design time, loops too slow to function, and aggregate
effects nobody inspects. Almost every proposed safeguard addresses mechanism
two. That mismatch is why "we have human oversight" so often turns out, after
something goes wrong, to have been true and irrelevant at the same time.
</Claim>

## Meaningful control has three parts, and checkbox oversight has none of them

The most useful idea from the safety literature comes out of the autonomous
weapons debate, where the stakes forced people to be precise. **Meaningful human
control** is not a person being present. It requires that a human can *track*
what the system is doing — understand enough to know when it is going wrong —
and that outcomes can be *traced* to a person who could have acted otherwise.

<Sidenote>
Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven, "Meaningful Human Control over Autonomous
Systems" (2018). The tracking/tracing pair maps almost exactly onto the
three-question test on the <a href="/topics/responsibility">responsibility</a>
page: information and time give you tracking, standing gives you tracing.
</Sidenote>

This is worth holding onto because it is falsifiable. Point at any deployment
and ask whether a specific person can track it and whether outcomes trace to
them. Most arrangements that describe themselves as having human oversight fail
both, and the failure is visible in advance rather than only after an incident.

## Three things that do not work

Naming these matters as much as naming the fixes, because each is widely
believed and each absorbs effort that would otherwise go somewhere useful.

- **Human oversight as a requirement.** Mandating that a person review
  consequential decisions produces a person who clicks. The requirement is
  satisfiable without being met, and satisfying it is cheaper than meeting it.
  Any rule written this way will be complied with in the worst available manner.
- **Explainability.** A post-hoc explanation of why a system produced an output
  can be fluent, plausible and unrelated to the actual cause. Worse, a good
  explanation increases confidence whether or not the decision was right — it
  can make override *less* likely rather than more.
- **The off switch.** The ability to shut a system down is not the same as the
  ability to shut it down *given what now depends on it*. By the time a system is
  load-bearing, turning it off is itself the harmful act. The off-switch problem
  is organisational, not technical, and it arrives long before anyone thinks to
  test the switch.

## Where the line goes

The practical question is not "how much AI" but "which decisions are reserved."
Four properties make a decision a poor candidate for automation, and they
compound:

- **Irreversible.** No feedback loop can correct what cannot be undone.
- **Allocates rights or liberty.** Bail, benefits, immigration, employment,
  medical rationing. These are contested value judgments dressed as
  classification problems.
- **About a person, not a population.** Systems are optimised on aggregates and
  applied to individuals. The individual has no recourse to the average.
- **Silent when wrong.** If an error announces itself, the loop repairs it. If
  it does not, error accumulates as policy. This is the axis the
  <a href="/judging">judgment checklist</a> is built on.

A decision with three or four of these belongs in the reserved category —
decided by a person who can be named, with the machine advising rather than
disposing. A decision with none of them is usually fine to automate and often
better automated.

<Counter label="The strongest objection">
"You are arguing for human control as though human control were safe. In a
great many domains the automated system is measurably better — and the override
is the error. People reject algorithms after seeing them err once, while
tolerating human colleagues who err constantly, and that asymmetry kills
patients, denies good loans and keeps bad drivers on the road. A framework that
makes override easier and more frequent will, on the evidence, cause net harm in
exactly the fields where the stakes are highest."
</Counter>

This is correct and it is the reason the page is a taxonomy rather than a
campaign. Algorithm aversion is well documented: people abandon a statistical
aid after watching it fail once, even having seen it outperform the human
alternative. Reflexive override is a real and lethal failure mode, and "keep a
human in charge" is not a safety property.

What survives the objection is narrower and, I think, still load-bearing. The
case for deferring to a system rests on conditions that can be checked:
**the objective is agreed, the outcome is measurable, the system was validated on
a population that includes this case, and errors surface.** Where all four hold,
defer — and the human's job is to monitor the aggregate rather than second-guess
the instance. Where any fails — contested objectives, unmeasurable outcomes,
out-of-distribution cases, silent errors — the system is advising on a question
it cannot actually answer, and its fluency is not evidence. Most of the harm
people fear lives in that second set, and most of the deployment enthusiasm
lives in treating it like the first.

## What to do, at three altitudes

**As the person in the loop.** Run the
[three-question test](/topics/responsibility) before accepting the role at all:
information, time, standing. If any answer is no, say so in writing before
anything goes wrong rather than after. Record your own position before you see
the output. Distinguish, explicitly, between approving and reviewing.

**As someone who deploys.** Write the reserved list first and put it where it
can be cited. Build the delay in rather than asking humans to keep up. Give
override teeth: measure how often it is used, and treat a rate of zero as a
fault indicator rather than a success metric. Audit the distribution of
outcomes, not a sample of cases — mechanism six is invisible to case review by
construction. Stage deployments so that reversibility is preserved while you
still know least.

**As a citizen.** The contestability right is the one to push for: any
consequential automated decision about a person must come with a route to human
review that has the power to overturn it. Not an explanation — an overturning.
Demand incident reporting, because a field that cannot see its own failures
cannot improve, and the aviation comparison the
[judgment](/topics/judgment) page leans on works only because crashes are
investigated in public.

<Claim label="The short version">
Do not ask whether there is a human in the loop. Ask which of the six
mechanisms applies, whether a specific named person can track and trace this
decision, and whether the decision belongs in the reserved category at all. If
the answer to the last is yes and the answer to the middle is no, the
arrangement is unsafe no matter how many people are nominally involved.
</Claim>
