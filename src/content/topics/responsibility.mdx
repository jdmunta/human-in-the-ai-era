<Lede>
Delegation moves two things that we habitually treat as one: the *doing* and the
*answering for it*. Machines take the first eagerly and cannot take the second at
all. A model has no interests to set back, nothing to lose, and no capacity to
be sorry. So the answerability does not vanish when the work is delegated. It
stays in the room, looking for a human to attach to.
</Lede>

## The responsibility gap

The philosopher Andreas Matthias named the problem in 2004, before it was
urgent. As systems become more autonomous and more adaptive, we approach a
condition where *nobody* satisfies the traditional conditions for being held
responsible. The manufacturer could not have predicted the specific behaviour,
because the system learns. The operator could not have controlled it, because it
acts faster than review allows and in ways the operator does not understand. The
system itself is not a moral agent.

Harm occurs. The conditions for fair attribution are met by no one. That is the
gap.

<Sidenote>
Matthias, "The responsibility gap: Ascribing responsibility for the actions of
learning automata" (2004). Written about industrial robots; it reads now like it
was written last year.
</Sidenote>

Societies do not tolerate gaps like this for long. Blame is not a philosophical
luxury; it is load-bearing social infrastructure. When there is no principled
place to put it, it does not evaporate. It goes somewhere unprincipled.

## The moral crumple zone

Madeleine Clare Elish gave the phenomenon its name. In a car, a crumple zone is
the region engineered to absorb the force of an impact, protecting what is
behind it. In a highly automated system, the human operator frequently plays the
same role: they absorb the moral and legal force of a failure they had no
meaningful capacity to prevent, protecting the system, its designers and its
deployers from scrutiny.

The pattern is consistent. The operator is nominally in charge, structurally
powerless, and blamed first — [drawn as a chain here](/contrast).

<Sidenote>
Elish, "Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction"
(2019). The Three Mile Island and Air France 447 investigations are her
recurring examples of the pattern being named and then repeated.
</Sidenote>

You can see it forming now in ordinary offices. A model generates a risk
assessment, a hiring shortlist, a set of clinical recommendations, a piece of
production code. A person clicks approve — one of forty they will click that
afternoon, with no realistic capacity to reconstruct the reasoning behind any of
them. When something goes wrong, that click is the fact everyone points to. It
was a signature. It was never an oversight mechanism.

<Claim label="The claim">
"Human in the loop" is not a safeguard. It is a <em>slot</em>, and the slot is
satisfied by a person who can technically say no. Whether it protects anyone
depends entirely on three things nobody usually checks: whether the human has
the information to disagree, the time to disagree, and the standing to disagree
without penalty. Absent any one of them you have not built oversight. You have
built a crumple zone and installed a colleague in it.
</Claim>

## The three-question test

Before accepting a role in an automated decision — or designing one for someone
else — run this. It takes a minute and it is the most practically useful thing
on this page.

<Table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Question</th><th>If the answer is no</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Information.</strong> Can I reconstruct enough of why the system produced this to identify a specific reason it might be wrong?</td>
<td>You are rubber-stamping. Your approval carries no epistemic content and should carry no liability.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Time.</strong> Given my actual volume, do I have the minutes each decision genuinely requires?</td>
<td>The system is designed for throughput, not review. The review step is decorative and everyone above you knows it.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Standing.</strong> If I reject this, what happens to me? Have overrides here ever survived?</td>
<td>You have responsibility without authority — the exact definition of a crumple zone.</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</Table>

If any answer is no, the correct move is not to work harder at reviewing. It is
to say so, in writing, to whoever owns the process. That sentence is often
career-uncomfortable, which is precisely why it is the substantive act rather
than the diligent clicking.

## Many hands, and the softening of harm

There is a second mechanism, older than automation and amplified by it. Harm
mediated through a long chain of contributors is easier to cause, because each
link can honestly say they only did a part. Add a machine to the chain and it
absorbs the residual discomfort beautifully — it is the ideal recipient of blame
precisely because it cannot be hurt by receiving it.

"The model flagged it." "The system scored them out." "That's what the tool
recommended." These are not explanations. They are the sound of a decision
losing its author. Watch for the grammatical tell: when consequential sentences
in your organisation start losing their subjects, responsibility has already
diffused past the point where anyone can hold it.

<Counter label="The strongest objection">
"Outcomes are what matter, not the tidiness of blame. If an automated system
halves the error rate, insisting on a clearly accountable human is moral
bookkeeping that costs real lives at the margin. We accept diffuse
responsibility everywhere — in supply chains, in pharmaceuticals, in
governments — and we manage it with institutions rather than with individual
answerability. Demanding a nameable culprit is a psychological need, not a
safety mechanism."
</Counter>

The premise is right and the conclusion does not follow. Accountability is not
primarily about post-hoc punishment; it is the mechanism that generates the
*pre-hoc care* which produces the good outcomes in the first place. Systems that
nobody will answer for are systems that nobody stress-tests. The correct target
of the objection is *individual scapegoating*, and the fix is to place
accountability where the power actually sits — with the people who chose to
deploy the system, set its thresholds and staffed its review — rather than to
abandon accountability altogether.

## Where responsibility sits on the ladder

Responsibility is the one dimension where the ladder inverts. On every other
page, higher rungs mean more human involvement. Here, *the rung you operate on
does not change your answerability at all* — and the mismatch is the danger.
