<Lede>
A site that argues for judgment ought to demonstrate some. The fastest way to
find out whether an argument is load-bearing is to attack it properly, so this
page collects the seven strongest objections to the rest of the site. They are
stated as their best proponents would state them, not as convenient
approximations. Three of them we think are wrong. Four we think are at least
partly right, and one of those is close to fatal.
</Lede>

## 1. Comparative advantage does not promise you a living

*The objection.* Optimists invoke comparative advantage: even if machines are
better at everything, trade still makes it sensible for humans to do something.
True and irrelevant. The theorem determines allocation, not price. Horses
retained a comparative advantage after the combustion engine and their
population collapsed by roughly ninety per cent, because their maintenance cost
exceeded the value of their remaining tasks. If the cost of keeping a human
functioning in a developed economy exceeds the marginal value of the tasks left
to them, the theorem offers no protection whatsoever.

*Our assessment: correct, and the site agrees with it.* The
[work](/topics/work) page states this directly. The distinction from horses is
that humans are the consumers whose demand constitutes the economy and the
voters who set distributive rules — which makes the outcome political rather
than mechanical. That is not reassurance. It relocates the problem from
economics to politics, where it is harder, not easier.

## 2. This is a coordination problem dressed as a self-help problem

*The objection.* Nearly every practice on this site is individually costly and
collectively futile. You cannot preserve your judgment by scheduling unaided
work if your employer measures output per hour and your competitor does not
bother. Personal discipline against a structural incentive is a losing position
held with dignity. The site knows the problem is structural and keeps prescribing
individual behaviour, because individual behaviour is what a website can
plausibly ask for.

*Our assessment: largely correct, and the most serious objection here.* Nothing
on this site solves a coordination problem. Two partial defences. First,
individual practice is what generates the constituency that eventually demands
structural change — the people who protect junior training are the people who
will argue for it institutionally. Second, some of it genuinely does pay off
individually: the unaided-first habit costs minutes and demonstrably reduces
commission error. But the honest position is that a personal protocol is not a
policy, and this site is much longer on the former.

## 3. It is a class artefact

*The objection.* Every recommendation assumes slack. Schedule manual reps.
Protect one unassisted domain. Leave the interstitials empty. Take a gap year and
build a portfolio. These are instructions for salaried professionals with
autonomy over their calendars. A warehouse worker on algorithmic dispatch, a
gig driver, a contractor paid per resolved ticket — none of them can decline the
efficient path. The people most exposed to automation have the least capacity to
follow any of this, and a site addressed to the people with options is doing
something other than what it claims.

*Our assessment: correct and largely unanswered.* The framing should be stated
plainly: most of this is written for people with discretion over how they work,
which is a minority and a privileged one. The extension to people without that
discretion is not a personal protocol at all — it is bargaining power, labour
organisation and regulation of algorithmic management. That is a different site,
and arguably the more important one.

## 4. The historical analogies cut against the alarm

*The objection.* Writing, printing, calculators and search were each met with
exactly these arguments about cognitive decline. Each time the predicted collapse
failed to materialise and the tool became invisible infrastructure. Doctors do
not diagnose from memory; nobody mourns. Pilots hand-fly far less than they did
and aviation is by an enormous margin the safest it has ever been — the very
example the [judgment](/topics/judgment) page leans on shows automation winning
on outcomes. The base rate for this genre of worry is poor.

*Our assessment: this is the best objection and it is only partly answerable.*
The base rate really is poor and intellectual humility is warranted. The
distinction we would defend is narrow: previous tools automated *specific
capacities* while leaving the general faculty of judgment untouched and, usually,
more in demand. This one targets the general faculty. Whether that difference is
real or whether it is what every generation believes about its own technology is
not something we can currently establish. We think it is real. We would not bet
heavily.

## 5. Preserving difficulty is nostalgia with a rationale

*The objection.* The desirable-difficulty literature concerns *learning* a skill,
not *performing* one. Extending it into a general principle that friction is good
is a leap the research does not license. Much of the atrophy evidence — the GPS
studies, the search-engine memory work — is correlational, modest, and prone to
the interpretation that the capacity was reallocated rather than lost.
Deliberately doing things the slow way when a better method exists is a hobby,
and giving it an epistemic justification does not change what it is.

*Our assessment: half right, and it correctly identifies a real risk of
puritanism.* The claim we would keep is deliberately narrow: for the specific
set of skills that are both generative *and* required to evaluate their own
output, atrophy is not a free trade. Outside that set — which is most skills —
the objection wins and the correct advice is to automate without guilt. The
[cognitive fitness](/topics/cognitive-fitness) page tries to state the test
sharply enough to be falsifiable. It could be sharper.

## 6. Meaning-without-contribution may not survive contact with reality

*The objection.* The [meaning](/topics/meaning) page argues the work-meaning bond
is historically contingent and therefore dissolvable. Contingent is not the same
as optional. Many deep human arrangements are contingent and load-bearing
anyway. Marienthal is cited as evidence for the latent deprivation model, but it
is equally evidence that when work disappears, people do not construct
alternative sources of meaning — they become apathetic and lose the capacity to
use their time at all. The site reads that finding as a design brief. It could
just as easily be read as a prognosis.

*Our assessment: a fair reading of the same evidence, and unresolved.* The
difference between the readings is whether the Marienthal villagers lacked
meaning or lacked *the material and institutional conditions* under which
meaning can be constructed — they were also, crucially, destitute and
stigmatised. We think the conditions mattered, which is why the
[civilization](/topics/civilization) page insists that distribution and meaning
are the same problem. But this is an interpretation, not a finding, and we are
not entitled to more confidence than that.

## 7. Or the whole thing is overblown

*The objection.* Capability may plateau. Scaling returns may flatten, reliability
may prove harder than fluency, deployment may stall for years on integration,
liability, regulation and simple organisational inertia — as almost every major
technology has. In a decade this site may read like the 1999 literature on how
the internet would dissolve the nation state. The safest prediction about
transformative-technology commentary is that it overestimates five years and
underestimates twenty.

*Our assessment: entirely possible, and the site is built to survive it.* This is
why the practices are framed as things that are good for you regardless: forming
your own view before consulting others, keeping a domain deep enough to detect
error, staying answerable for what you sign, protecting attention, being needed
by someone. If capability plateaus tomorrow, none of that was wasted. That was a
deliberate design constraint, and it is the closest thing the site has to a
hedge.

## What survives

Stripping out everything the objections above successfully damage, this is what
we think remains defensible.

<Claim label="The minimal claim">
<strong>One.</strong> A specific and identifiable subset of cognitive skills —
those that are generative of other capacities and required to evaluate their own
output — do not degrade gracefully when delegated, and maintaining them requires
deliberate scheduled practice rather than good intentions.

<strong>Two.</strong> Accountability cannot be delegated to a system that cannot
be answerable, so it lands on a human regardless of whether that human had the
information, time or standing to exercise it. Checking those three things before
accepting the role is always worth doing.

<strong>Three.</strong> Meaning has more sources than economic contribution, but
they require structure, consequence and being depended upon — none of which
arrive automatically with free time or with money.

<strong>Four.</strong> The distributional question determines more about how this
goes than any individual's skill choices, and treating it as somebody else's
department is the characteristic error of writing in this genre — this site
included.
</Claim>

Everything else on this site is argument, and argument can be wrong. If you find
a better objection than the seven here, it belongs on this page.
